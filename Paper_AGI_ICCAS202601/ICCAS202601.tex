\title{Language-Based Calibration and Bayesian Optimization for\\ MPC Weight Tuning in Vehicle Slalom Simulation}

\author{Kyoungtae Ji${}^{1}$, Dongyeol Won${}^{1}$,  and Kyoungseok Han${}^{1*}$}

\affils{${}^{1}$Department of Automotive Engineering, Hanyang University,\\
Seoul, Korea (kyoungsh@hanyang.ac.kr) {\small${}^{*}$ Corresponding author}}

\abstract{
This paper presents a simulator-in-the-loop study of model predictive control (MPC) weight calibration for an autonomous vehicle slalom maneuver. The controller structure is fixed, and the optimization target is a four-dimensional set of MPC weights governing lateral tracking, heading response, steering effort, and steering smoothness. Each candidate is evaluated by a closed-loop CarMaker-Simulink simulation and converted into a simulation objective including road departure, evaluated cone contacts, lateral tracking error, and heading error. Additional steering and yaw metrics are logged for diagnostic analysis. The study compares non-adaptive sampling, Bayesian optimization (BO), and LLM-based candidate proposal under a common 50-trial budget, and uses Sobol landscape analysis to interpret the resulting search behavior. Results show complementary behavior: LLM-based calibration rapidly reaches physically plausible hit-free regions and performs effective local refinement, whereas BO and Sobol sampling reveal non-intuitive feasible regions that language-only search tends not to explore.
}

\keywords{
Bayesian optimization, large language model, model predictive control, autonomous vehicle simulation, slalom maneuver

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Model predictive control (MPC) is widely used for vehicle control because it can handle multi-objective tracking and actuation constraints in a systematic way. In vehicle applications, control-oriented models are often kept simpler than the high-fidelity plant so that they can be used repeatedly inside an online optimizer or estimator \cite{rajamani2006vehicle,reina2017vehicle,maull2022optimizing}. However, even when the controller structure is fixed, its practical performance depends strongly on the selection of weighting parameters. In high-fidelity simulation, these parameters are often tuned by repeated trial-and-error, which is time-consuming and difficult to reproduce.

This paper considers MPC weight tuning as a black-box optimization problem. A candidate weight vector is applied to the controller, one closed-loop slalom simulation is executed, and the resulting driving performance is summarized by a simulation objective. This formulation is suitable for Bayesian optimization (BO), especially when each evaluation is expensive and derivative information is unavailable. Similar simulation-based optimization ideas have been used in automotive calibration and racing-line computation, where candidate designs are evaluated by a simulator or numerical vehicle model rather than by analytic gradients \cite{jain2020computing,arya2019fully,eriksson2019scalable}.

Recent large language models (LLMs) have shown strong ability to summarize logs, reason over numerical trends, and suggest experimental directions. Recent studies have also investigated LLMs as assistants for BO, scientific experimentation, and autonomous-driving reasoning \cite{liu2024large,cisse2025language,cisse2026can,cui2026llm4ad}. In this work, the LLM is not treated as a direct steering controller. Instead, it is used as an optimization assistant that can interpret previous trials, diagnose failure patterns, and propose candidate regions or warm-start points. This preserves the MPC controller as the low-level control law while investigating whether LLM reasoning can improve the sample efficiency of simulator-based tuning.

The central question is not whether language reasoning replaces numerical optimization. Instead, we ask how language-based calibration behaves differently from BO in a physical closed-loop tuning task. The slalom benchmark is useful for this comparison because feasible hit-free regions can be narrow and sometimes counterintuitive in the controller-weight space. This creates a setting where physically plausible LLM proposals may reach good solutions quickly, while algorithmic exploration may still be needed to reveal separated or non-intuitive local regions.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A vehicle slalom MPC weight-tuning task is formulated as a simulator-in-the-loop black-box optimization problem, and a Sobol search-space audit is used to analyze the sparsity and separation of hit-free regions in the four-dimensional log-scaled weight space.
    \item A unified repeated-experiment protocol is presented for comparing LHC, random search, BO, and LLM-based search under the same search range, objective function, and simulation budget.
    \item LLM-based candidate proposal is experimentally evaluated in the tuning loop, where the LLM selects the next MPC weights from vehicle context, driving-task information, and previous trial logs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation and Objective}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Vehicle Dynamics Model}
\label{subsec:vehicle_model}

The MPC controller uses a linearized lateral bicycle model around a constant
longitudinal speed. This class of low-order lateral model is commonly used when
vehicle yaw and lateral motion must be represented with a small number of
states for estimation or control \cite{rajamani2006vehicle,reina2017vehicle}. Let
the state vector be
\begin{equation}
x =
\begin{bmatrix}
v_y & r & e_y & e_\psi
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},
\label{eq:state_vector}
\end{equation}
where $v_y$ is the lateral velocity, $r$ is the yaw rate, $e_y$ is the lateral
tracking error with respect to the reference path, and $e_\psi$ is the heading
error. The MPC has a single control input, the steering-wheel angle,
\begin{equation}
u = \delta_{\mathrm{sw}}.
\label{eq:control_input}
\end{equation}
Note that longitudinal speed control is not included in the optimization. In the
closed-loop evaluation, the plant is the high-fidelity CarMaker vehicle model,
whereas the bicycle model is used only as the MPC prediction model.

The continuous-time model is written as
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}(t) = A_c x(t) + B_c u(t),
\label{eq:continuous_model}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
A_c =
\begin{bmatrix}
a_1 & a_2 & 0 & 0 \\
a_3 & a_4 & 0 & 0 \\
1   & 0   & 0 & V_x \\
0   & 1   & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
B_c =
\begin{bmatrix}
b_1 \\ b_2 \\ 0 \\ 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:continuous_matrices}
\end{equation}
The coefficients are
\begin{align}
a_1 &= -\frac{2C_f+2C_r}{mV_x}, \nonumber\\
a_2 &= -\frac{2C_f l_f-2C_r l_r}{mV_x} - V_x, \nonumber\\
a_3 &= -\frac{2C_f l_f-2C_r l_r}{I_z V_x}, \nonumber\\
a_4 &= -\frac{2C_f l_f^2+2C_r l_r^2}{I_z V_x}, \nonumber\\
b_1 &= \frac{2C_f}{m}, \qquad
b_2 = \frac{2C_f l_f}{I_z},
\label{eq:model_coefficients}
\end{align}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $l_f$ and
$l_r$ are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axles,
$C_f$ and $C_r$ are the front and rear cornering stiffnesses, and $V_x$ is the
nominal longitudinal speed. The tracking output vector used by the MPC is
\begin{equation}
y =
\begin{bmatrix}
e_y & e_\psi
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
= Cx,
\quad
C =
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:output_equation}
\end{equation}
The model is discretized with sampling time $T_s$ as
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = A_d x_k + B_d u_k,\qquad y_k = Cx_k.
\label{eq:discrete_model}
\end{equation}
\subsection{MPC Formulation}
\label{subsec:mpc_formulation}

At each sampling instant, the MPC predicts the future output trajectory over a
prediction horizon $N_p$ and computes a control sequence over a control horizon
$N_c$. Following standard automotive MPC practice, actuator constraints and
tracking objectives are handled in a finite-horizon optimization problem
\cite{maull2022optimizing}. Because the path-following task is expressed in
terms of lateral and heading errors relative to the reference path, the nominal
output reference is
\begin{equation}
y^{\mathrm{ref}}_k =
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},
\label{eq:reference_output}
\end{equation}
where the two components correspond to zero lateral and heading tracking error.

The finite-horizon quadratic program is
\begin{align}
\min_{\{u_{k+i|k}\}} \quad
& \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}
\left\| y_{k+i|k} - y^{\mathrm{ref}}_{k+i} \right\|_Q^2
\nonumber\\
&+
\sum_{i=0}^{N_c-1}
\left(
\left\| u_{k+i|k} \right\|_R^2
+
\left\| \Delta u_{k+i|k} \right\|_{R_\Delta}^2
\right)
\label{eq:mpc_qp}
\end{align}
subject to
\begin{align}
x_{k+i+1|k} &= A_d x_{k+i|k} + B_d u_{k+i|k}, \nonumber\\
y_{k+i|k} &= Cx_{k+i|k}, \nonumber\\
u_{\min} &\leq u_{k+i|k} \leq u_{\max}, \nonumber\\
\Delta u_{\min} &\leq \Delta u_{k+i|k} \leq \Delta u_{\max},
\label{eq:mpc_constraints}
\end{align}
The input and input-rate constraints represent physical limits on the steering
angle and steering angular velocity.

The weighting matrices are diagonal:
\begin{equation}
Q = \mathrm{diag}(q_y,q_\psi), \quad
R = r_\delta, \quad
R_\Delta = r_{\Delta\delta}.
\label{eq:mpc_weights}
\end{equation}
Here, $q_y$ and $q_\psi$ weight lateral and heading tracking errors,
respectively, while $r_\delta$ and $r_{\Delta\delta}$ weight steering effort and
steering-rate variation.

\subsection{MPC Weight Tuning Problem}
\label{subsec:mpc_weights}

The outer-loop calibration problem tunes these four MPC weights while keeping
the controller structure, horizons, prediction model, and steering constraints
fixed. This focuses the comparison on sample-efficient weight selection rather
than on controller redesign.

The optimization target is the MPC weight vector
\begin{equation}
\theta =
\left[
q_y,\,
q_\psi,\,
r_\delta,\,
r_{\Delta\delta}
\right]^\top ,
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
with the components defined in \eqref{eq:mpc_weights}. Each candidate
$\theta$ specifies one complete MPC weight setting to be evaluated by
closed-loop simulation.

The tuning problem is written as
\begin{equation}
\theta^\star =
\arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} J(\theta),
\label{eq:optimization_problem}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta$ denotes the feasible search space and $J(\theta)$ is obtained
from one closed-loop simulation. The feasible set $\Theta$ spans
$[0.01,100]^4$ on a logarithmic scale, and each sample is decoded to the
corresponding MPC weight before simulation. This defines an expensive black-box
calibration problem: each candidate weight vector requires a closed-loop
simulation, as in simulation-based automotive calibration and racing-line
optimization tasks \cite{jain2020computing,arya2019fully}.

\subsection{Simulation-Based Objective}
\label{subsec:objective}

The simulation objective is designed to penalize road departure and evaluated cone
contacts first, and then rank runs by lateral and heading tracking accuracy. For
a candidate weight vector $\theta$, let the simulator return a finite time
series over $t \in [0,T]$.

The simulation objective is
\begin{align}
J(\theta)
&=
w_{\mathrm{fail}} I_{\mathrm{fail}}
+ w_{\mathrm{hit}} N_{\mathrm{hit}} \nonumber\\
&\quad
+ w_y\,\mathrm{RMSE}(e_y)
+ w_{\infty}\,\|e_y\|_{\infty} \nonumber\\
&\quad
+ w_\psi\,\mathrm{RMSE}(e_\psi).
\label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
Here $I_{\mathrm{fail}}$ is one when the vehicle departs from the road and zero
otherwise. $N_{\mathrm{hit}}$ denotes the number of evaluated cone contacts;
entry contacts before the evaluation gate are ignored. The tracking variables
$e_y$ and $e_\psi$ are the lateral and heading errors computed from the
simulation output.

\subsection{Simulator-in-the-Loop Evaluation}
\label{subsec:sil}

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the common evaluation loop used by all search
methods. A candidate-selection method proposes an MPC weight vector, the weights
are applied to the Simulink MPC controller, and the CarMaker-Simulink
closed-loop slalom simulation returns an objective value and diagnostic trial
log. The result is appended to the history and used by the next
candidate-selection step.

The history after $n$ evaluations is
\begin{equation}
D_n =
\{(\theta_1,J_1),\ldots,(\theta_n,J_n)\}.
\label{eq:dataset}
\end{equation}
All candidate-selection methods use the same simulation objective and metric
extractor. This makes the comparison focus on how the next candidate is selected
rather than on differences in the simulator or post-processing pipeline.

\subsection{Candidate-Selection Strategies}
\label{subsec:candidate_selection}

The compared methods differ only in how they choose the next candidate
$\theta_{n+1}$ from the common search space. LHC generates a space-filling
Latin-hypercube design in the normalized logarithmic weight space. Random search
draws independent samples from the same normalized search space. These two
methods provide non-adaptive baselines.

BO begins with LHC-based space-filling samples to initialize the search and then
fits a Gaussian-process surrogate model to the observed history $D_n$. The next
candidate is selected by maximizing an acquisition function:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{n+1} =
\arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta}
a(\theta;D_n).
\label{eq:acquisition}
\end{equation}
The implementation uses expected improvement evaluated over a finite candidate
pool that includes both global random candidates and local perturbations around
the current best observation.

The LLM-based method uses the previous trial table, best-so-far metrics, task
context, and fixed search bounds to propose the next candidate. It does not use
a surrogate model, so its performance indicates whether language-based reasoning
alone can provide useful tuning suggestions.

In addition to these 50-trial candidate-selection methods, Sobol
low-discrepancy sampling is used as a separate non-adaptive landscape audit. It
is not treated as an optimizer baseline. Instead, it probes whether hit-free
candidates occupy sparse or separated regions of the log-scaled four-dimensional
search space.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Slalom Simulation Scenario}
\label{subsec:scenario}

The benchmark scenario is derived from the CarMaker Nominal Slalom example. The
reference path is taken from the Manual Drive Maneuver trajectory of the base
CarMaker slalom example, which completes the maneuver without cone contacts. To
make the tuning task more discriminative, the target speed is increased by 5\%
to 61 km/h; under this modified condition, the CarMaker IPGDriver baseline
produces two cone contacts.

In the MPC experiments, longitudinal driving is handled by IPGDriver, while the
Simulink MPC controller provides the lateral steering input in closed-loop
co-simulation with IPG CarMaker. The MPC output is the steering-wheel angle, and
all methods use the same vehicle, road, reference path, controller structure,
and physical steering constraints.

\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{subsec:experimental_design}

The main comparison evaluates LHC, random search, BO, and LLM-based search
under the same 50-trial simulation budget. Each method is repeated five times
with independent stochastic settings when applicable. BO uses the first 15
trials as an LHC initial design and performs acquisition-based updates for the
remaining 35 trials. The other methods select all 50 candidates according to the
candidate-selection rules described in Section~\ref{subsec:candidate_selection}.

For LLM-based search, GPT-5.5 medium is used through Codex. The agent is given
MCP tools that can operate MATLAB, Simulink, and CarMaker through the experiment
interface, read trial logs, and write candidate decisions. The LLM does not
receive the closed-form simulation objective; it observes only the available
trial results and diagnostic metrics.

Sobol sampling is run separately as a landscape audit and is not counted as a
50-trial optimizer baseline.

In all experiments, the objective weights are set to
\begin{equation}
(w_{\mathrm{fail}},w_{\mathrm{hit}},w_y,w_{\infty},w_\psi)
=
(100,10,4,0.5,5).
\label{eq:objective_weights}
\end{equation}
These weights prioritize road departure and cone contacts before ranking
completed runs by tracking accuracy.

In addition to the simulation objective, the results report cone contacts,
road-departure events, lateral and heading tracking errors, steering response,
and yaw-rate diagnostics.

The results show different strengths. LLM-based calibration found a hit-free

The repeated runs also show different reproducibility patterns in the weight
space. The three successful BO repetitions did not converge to the same
parameter set. Their best hit-free solutions appeared in separated regions,
including one aggressive high-heading solution and two low-heading solutions
with different steering-penalty combinations. In contrast, the four successful
LLM-based repetitions remained within a more consistent high-heading family:
the exact weights differed across repetitions, but all successful LLM-based

relatively small steering penalties. Thus, LLM-based calibration is more
reproducible in terms of the type of controller setting it selects, whereas BO
is more diverse and can reveal qualitatively different local optima.

\subsection{Landscape Analysis}
\label{subsec:landscape_tolerance}

Sobol sampling was added to interpret the above behavior. In this analysis,
1024 Sobol samples were evaluated, with 775 simulation completions and two
hit-free completions. Both hit-free Sobol samples belong to a
low-heading-weight family, with $q_\psi$ close to the lower end of the search
range. Similar low-heading hit-free solutions were also observed in LHC and
BO runs. This family is not a single numerical outlier, but nearby samples often
produce one or more cone hits, indicating a narrow feasible region.

By contrast, the LLM-based successful solutions occupy a more physically
interpretable high-heading family. Across 101 hit-free LLM-based trials, the
successful weights used moderate-to-high lateral and heading weights and low to
moderate steering penalties. Thus, LLM-based search is effective at exploiting a
plausible controller region, but it is less likely to select the low-heading
branch that BO and Sobol sampling can reveal.

physically plausible controller setting. In contrast, BO produced several

low-heading solutions with different steering-penalty combinations. These
representatives are not additional methods; they summarize distinct weight
patterns observed among the hit-free candidates.

The overall result therefore supports a limited but useful conclusion. LLM-based
calibration is effective as a fast feasibility-seeking and local-refinement
mechanism. BO is less aligned with physical intuition but can find lower-cost
or more diverse local regions. These observations motivate future hybrid
methods, but the present paper focuses on the behavior of LLM-based calibration
and BO rather than claiming a completed hybrid optimizer.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper formulates MPC weight tuning for a vehicle slalom maneuver as a simulator-in-the-loop black-box optimization problem. The proposed framework evaluates each candidate through closed-loop simulation, computes a simulation objective from road departure, evaluated cone contacts, and tracking metrics, and logs yaw and steering metrics for diagnostic analysis under a common candidate-selection budget.

The nominal slalom results indicate that LLM-based search can rapidly find feasible hit-free MPC weights and refine physically plausible controller settings, while BO can discover lower-objective and less intuitive regions but with lower feasibility frequency. The Sobol analysis indicates that the objective landscape contains multiple local minimum regions: hit-free solutions appear in both high-heading regions favored by LLM-based search and low-heading regions that are easier for non-language exploration to reveal. Future work will focus on exploration-preserving hybrid designs, low-friction scenarios, additional maneuvers, and reinforcement learning only if the task is reformulated as a policy-learning problem.
