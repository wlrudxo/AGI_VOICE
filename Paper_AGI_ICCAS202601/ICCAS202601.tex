\title{Simulator-in-the-Loop MPC Weight Tuning Using\\ LLM-Based Search and Bayesian Optimization}

\author{Kyoungtae Ji${}^{1}$, Dongyeol Won${}^{1}$,  and Kyoungseok Han${}^{1*}$}

\affils{${}^{1}$Department of Automotive Engineering, Hanyang University,\\
Seoul, Korea (kyoungsh@hanyang.ac.kr) {\small${}^{*}$ Corresponding author}}

\abstract{
This paper presents a simulator-in-the-loop study of model predictive control (MPC) weight calibration for autonomous vehicle path-following using a high-fidelity closed-loop simulation environment. With the controller structure fixed, the optimization target is a four-dimensional MPC weight vector for lateral tracking, heading response, steering effort, and steering smoothness. Each candidate is evaluated by a simulation objective that penalizes road departure, counted cone contacts, and tracking error. Under a common 50-trial budget, the study compares Latin-hypercube sampling, random search, Bayesian optimization (BO), and LLM-based search over five independent repetitions. The results show complementary behavior: LLM-based search reaches hit-free candidates early in four of five repetitions, while BO finds the lowest objective value when its exploration reaches a useful region. A 4096-sample Sobol-based landscape analysis finds only seven hit-free samples, showing a low hit-free fraction among the evaluated samples. These results suggest that LLM-based search is effective for rapid feasibility seeking in this task, whereas numerical exploration remains important for discovering less intuitive weight regions.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Model predictive control (MPC) is widely used for vehicle control because it can handle multi-objective tracking and actuation constraints in a systematic way. In vehicle applications, control-oriented models are often kept simpler than the high-fidelity plant so that they can be used repeatedly inside an online optimizer or estimator \cite{rajamani2006vehicle,reina2017vehicle,maull2022optimizing}. However, even when the controller structure is fixed, its practical performance depends strongly on the selection of weighting parameters. In high-fidelity simulation, these parameters are often tuned by repeated trial-and-error, which is time-consuming and difficult to reproduce.

This paper considers MPC weight tuning as a black-box optimization problem. A candidate weight vector is applied to the controller, one closed-loop path-following simulation is executed, and the resulting driving performance is summarized by a simulation objective. This formulation is suitable for Bayesian optimization (BO), especially when each evaluation is expensive and derivative information is unavailable. Similar simulation-based optimization ideas have been used in automotive calibration and racing-line computation, where candidate designs are evaluated by a simulator or numerical vehicle model rather than by analytic gradients \cite{jain2020computing,arya2019fully}.

Recent large language models (LLMs) have shown strong ability to summarize logs, reason over numerical trends, and suggest experimental directions. Recent studies have also investigated LLMs as assistants for BO and closed-loop scientific experimentation \cite{liu2024large,cisse2025language,cisse2026can}. In this work, the LLM is not treated as a direct steering controller. Instead, it is used as an optimization assistant that can interpret previous trials, diagnose failure patterns, and propose candidate regions or warm-start points. This preserves the MPC controller as the low-level control law while investigating whether LLM reasoning can improve the sample efficiency of simulator-based tuning.

The central question is not whether language reasoning replaces numerical optimization, but how language-based calibration behaves differently from BO in a physical closed-loop tuning task. A constrained path-following benchmark is useful here because feasible contact-free regions can be narrow and sometimes counterintuitive in the weight space: intuitive LLM proposals may reach good solutions quickly, while algorithmic exploration may still be needed to reveal separated or non-intuitive local regions.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A vehicle path-following MPC weight-tuning task is formulated as a simulator-in-the-loop black-box optimization problem, with a Sobol-based landscape analysis used to examine the sparsity of hit-free candidates in the four-dimensional log-scaled weight space.
    \item A matched repeated-experiment evaluation is conducted for LHC, random search, BO, and LLM-based search under the same search range, simulation objective, and 50-trial budget.
    \item Unlike prior work coupling an LLM to a BO loop, the LLM is used as a standalone sequential candidate proposer and compared against BO under an identical simulator budget, separating feasibility discovery from objective minimization.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Formulation and Objective}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Vehicle Dynamics Model}
\label{subsec:vehicle_model}

The MPC controller uses a linearized lateral bicycle model around a constant
longitudinal speed. This class of low-order lateral model is commonly used when
vehicle yaw and lateral motion must be represented with a small number of
states for estimation or control \cite{rajamani2006vehicle,reina2017vehicle}. Let
the state vector be
\begin{equation}
x =
\begin{bmatrix}
v_y & r & e_y & e_\psi
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},
\label{eq:state_vector}
\end{equation}
where $v_y$ is the lateral velocity, $r$ is the yaw rate, $e_y$ is the lateral
tracking error with respect to the reference path, and $e_\psi$ is the heading
error. The MPC has a single control input, the steering-wheel angle,
\begin{equation}
u = \delta_{\mathrm{sw}}.
\label{eq:control_input}
\end{equation}
Note that longitudinal speed control is not included in the optimization. In the
closed-loop evaluation, the plant is a high-fidelity vehicle simulation model,
whereas the bicycle model is used only as the MPC prediction model.

The continuous-time model is written as
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}(t) = A_c x(t) + B_c u(t),
\label{eq:continuous_model}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
A_c =
\begin{bmatrix}
a_1 & a_2 & 0 & 0 \\
a_3 & a_4 & 0 & 0 \\
1   & 0   & 0 & V_x \\
0   & 1   & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
B_c =
\begin{bmatrix}
b_1 \\ b_2 \\ 0 \\ 0
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:continuous_matrices}
\end{equation}
The coefficients are
\begin{align}
a_1 &= -\frac{2C_f+2C_r}{mV_x}, \nonumber\\
a_2 &= -\frac{2C_f l_f-2C_r l_r}{mV_x} - V_x, \nonumber\\
a_3 &= -\frac{2C_f l_f-2C_r l_r}{I_z V_x}, \nonumber\\
a_4 &= -\frac{2C_f l_f^2+2C_r l_r^2}{I_z V_x}, \nonumber\\
b_1 &= \frac{2C_f}{m}, \qquad
b_2 = \frac{2C_f l_f}{I_z},
\label{eq:model_coefficients}
\end{align}
where $m$ is the vehicle mass, $I_z$ is the yaw moment of inertia, $l_f$ and
$l_r$ are the distances from the center of gravity to the front and rear axles,
$C_f$ and $C_r$ are the front and rear cornering stiffnesses, and $V_x$ is the
nominal longitudinal speed. The tracking output vector used by the MPC is
\begin{equation}
y =
\begin{bmatrix}
e_y & e_\psi
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
= Cx,
\quad
C =
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:output_equation}
\end{equation}
The model is discretized with sampling time $T_s$ as
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1} = A_d x_k + B_d u_k,\qquad y_k = Cx_k.
\label{eq:discrete_model}
\end{equation}
\subsection{MPC Formulation}
\label{subsec:mpc_formulation}

At each sampling instant, the MPC predicts the future output trajectory over a
prediction horizon $N_p$ and computes a control sequence over a control horizon
$N_c$. Following standard automotive MPC practice, actuator constraints and
tracking objectives are handled in a finite-horizon optimization problem
\cite{maull2022optimizing}. Because the path-following task is expressed in
terms of lateral and heading errors relative to the reference path, the nominal
output reference is
\begin{equation}
y^{\mathrm{ref}}_k =
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},
\label{eq:reference_output}
\end{equation}
where the two components correspond to zero lateral and heading tracking error.

The finite-horizon quadratic program is
\begin{align}
\min_{\{u_{k+i|k}\}} \quad
& \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}
\left\| y_{k+i|k} - y^{\mathrm{ref}}_{k+i} \right\|_Q^2
\nonumber\\
&+
\sum_{i=0}^{N_c-1}
\left(
\left\| u_{k+i|k} \right\|_R^2
+
\left\| \Delta u_{k+i|k} \right\|_{R_\Delta}^2
\right)
\label{eq:mpc_qp}
\end{align}
subject to
\begin{align}
x_{k+i+1|k} &= A_d x_{k+i|k} + B_d u_{k+i|k}, \nonumber\\
y_{k+i|k} &= Cx_{k+i|k}, \nonumber\\
u_{\min} &\leq u_{k+i|k} \leq u_{\max}, \nonumber\\
\Delta u_{\min} &\leq \Delta u_{k+i|k} \leq \Delta u_{\max},
\label{eq:mpc_constraints}
\end{align}
The input and input-rate constraints represent physical limits on the steering
angle and steering angular velocity.

The weighting matrices are diagonal:
\begin{equation}
Q = \mathrm{diag}(q_y,q_\psi), \quad
R = r_\delta, \quad
R_\Delta = r_{\Delta\delta}.
\label{eq:mpc_weights}
\end{equation}
Here, $q_y$ and $q_\psi$ weight lateral and heading tracking errors,
respectively, while $r_\delta$ and $r_{\Delta\delta}$ weight steering effort and
steering-rate variation.

\subsection{MPC Weight Tuning Problem}
\label{subsec:mpc_weights}

The outer-loop calibration problem tunes these four MPC weights while keeping
the controller structure, horizons, prediction model, and steering constraints
fixed. This focuses the comparison on sample-efficient weight selection rather
than on controller redesign.

The optimization target is the MPC weight vector
\begin{equation}
\theta =
\left[
q_y,\,
q_\psi,\,
r_\delta,\,
r_{\Delta\delta}
\right]^\top ,
\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
with the components defined in \eqref{eq:mpc_weights}. Each candidate
$\theta$ specifies one complete MPC weight setting to be evaluated by
closed-loop simulation.

The tuning problem is written as
\begin{equation}
\theta^\star =
\arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} J(\theta),
\label{eq:optimization_problem}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta$ denotes the feasible search space and $J(\theta)$ is obtained
from one closed-loop simulation. The feasible set $\Theta$ spans
$[0.01,100]^4$ on a logarithmic scale, and each sample is decoded to the
corresponding MPC weight before simulation. Each candidate thus requires one
closed-loop simulation, as in simulation-based automotive calibration and
racing-line optimization tasks \cite{jain2020computing,arya2019fully}.

\subsection{Simulation-Based Objective}
\label{subsec:objective}

The simulation objective is designed to penalize road departure and counted cone
contacts first, and then rank runs by lateral and heading tracking accuracy. For
a candidate weight vector $\theta$, let the simulator return a finite time
series over $t \in [0,T]$.

The simulation objective is
\begin{align}
J(\theta)
&=
w_{\mathrm{fail}} I_{\mathrm{fail}}
+ w_{\mathrm{hit}} N_{\mathrm{hit}} \nonumber\\
&\quad
+ w_y\,\mathrm{RMSE}(e_y)
+ w_{\infty}\,\|e_y\|_{\infty} \nonumber\\
&\quad
+ w_\psi\,\mathrm{RMSE}(e_\psi).
\label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
Here $I_{\mathrm{fail}}$ is one when the simulation terminates early due to
road departure, and zero otherwise. Cone contacts are detected by the
simulation scenario, and each contact is recorded with its road position;
$N_{\mathrm{hit}}$ counts contacts at or beyond the first slalom gate,
excluding the entry section used to converge onto the reference path from the
initial transient. The
tracking variables $e_y$ and $e_\psi$ are the lateral and heading errors
computed from the simulation output.

\subsection{Simulator-in-the-Loop Evaluation}
\label{subsec:sil}

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the common evaluation loop used by all search
methods. A candidate-selection method proposes an MPC weight vector, the weights
are applied to the MPC controller, and the high-fidelity closed-loop
path-following simulation returns an objective value and diagnostic trial
log. The result is appended to the history and used by the next
candidate-selection step.

The history after $n$ evaluations is
\begin{equation}
D_n =
\{(\theta_1,J_1),\ldots,(\theta_n,J_n)\}.
\label{eq:dataset}
\end{equation}
All methods use the same simulation objective and metric extractor, so the
comparison focuses on how the next candidate is selected rather than on
differences in the simulator or post-processing pipeline.

\subsection{Candidate-Selection Strategies}
\label{subsec:candidate_selection}

The compared methods differ only in how they choose the next candidate
$\theta_{n+1}$ from the common search space. LHC generates a space-filling
Latin-hypercube design in the normalized logarithmic weight space
\cite{mckay2000comparison}. Random search draws independent samples from the
same normalized search space. These two methods provide non-adaptive baselines.

BO begins with LHC-based space-filling samples to initialize the search and then
fits a Gaussian-process surrogate model to the observed history $D_n$. The next
candidate is selected by maximizing an acquisition function:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{n+1} =
\arg\max_{\theta \in \Theta}
a(\theta;D_n).
\label{eq:acquisition}
\end{equation}
Expected improvement is used as the acquisition function in the same normalized
log-weight space used by the sampling baselines \cite{frazier2018tutorial}.

The LLM-based method uses the previous trial table, best-so-far metrics, task
context, and fixed search bounds to propose the next candidate. It does not use
a surrogate model, so its performance indicates whether language-based reasoning
alone can provide useful tuning suggestions.

In addition, Sobol low-discrepancy sampling \cite{sobol1967distribution} is
used as a separate non-adaptive landscape analysis, not an optimizer baseline;
it probes whether hit-free candidates occupy sparse or separated regions of the
log-scaled four-dimensional search space.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Slalom Simulation Scenario}
\label{subsec:scenario}

The general simulator-in-the-loop calibration workflow is instantiated in this
study using an IPG CarMaker-Simulink co-simulation benchmark. The benchmark
scenario is derived from the CarMaker Nominal Slalom example, with the
reference path taken from its Manual Drive Maneuver trajectory, which completes
the maneuver without cone contacts. To
make the tuning task more discriminative, the target speed is increased by 5\%
to 61 km/h; under this modified condition, the CarMaker IPGDriver baseline
produces two cone contacts.

In the MPC experiments, longitudinal driving is handled by IPGDriver, while the
Simulink MPC controller provides the lateral steering input in closed-loop
co-simulation with IPG CarMaker. The MPC output is the steering-wheel angle, and
all methods use the same vehicle, road, reference path, controller structure,
and physical steering constraints.



\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{subsec:experimental_design}

The main comparison evaluates LHC, random search, BO, and LLM-based search
under the same 50-trial simulation budget. Each method is repeated five times
with independent stochastic settings when applicable. BO uses the first 15
trials as an LHC initial design and performs acquisition-based updates for the
remaining 35 trials. The other methods select all 50 candidates according to the
candidate-selection rules described in Section~\ref{subsec:candidate_selection}.
The comparison concerns simulator-evaluation efficiency, not candidate-selection
time or cost, which differs between GP fitting and LLM inference.

The lateral controller is implemented with the standard Simulink MPC Controller
block using a MATLAB MPC object. The controller sample time is $T_s=0.02$ s,
with prediction and control horizons $N_p=40$ and $N_c=8$, respectively. The
steering-wheel angle and rate limits are $[-12,12]$ rad and $[-10,10]$ rad/s.

For LLM-based search, OpenAI GPT-5.5 with medium reasoning effort is used
through Codex with tools to operate the experiment interface, read trial logs,
and submit candidate decisions.
At each trial, the LLM observes logged outcomes, including previous MPC weights,
objective value, status, cone contacts, tracking errors, and diagnostic metrics,
but it is not given the analytic objective expression or penalty weights. It
then proposes one candidate of four decoded MPC weights per trial through the
same evaluation pipeline used by the other methods. The log-scale search bounds
are provided in the task context at every trial, and all LLM proposals across
the five repetitions remained within these bounds, so no clipping or rejection
was needed.

In all experiments, the objective weights are set to
\begin{equation}
(w_{\mathrm{fail}},w_{\mathrm{hit}},w_y,w_{\infty},w_\psi)
=
(100,10,4,0.5,5).
\label{eq:objective_weights}
\end{equation}
These weights prioritize road departure and cone contacts before ranking
completed runs by tracking accuracy.

In addition to the simulation objective, the results report cone contacts,
road-departure events, lateral and heading tracking errors, steering response,
and yaw-rate diagnostics.


\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This section compares fixed-budget search performance, best-run trajectories,
and hit-free weight-space patterns.

completed the simulation with zero counted cone contacts. The table also

The four methods exhibit distinct fixed-budget behavior. Random search does not
produce a hit-free best candidate in any repetition, and its median best
objective remains near the one-cone-contact level. LHC improves the space
coverage compared with random sampling, but only one repetition becomes hit-free,
and that occurs at the final trial. These results indicate that simple
non-adaptive coverage is not sufficient for reliable calibration under the
50-trial budget.

BO succeeds in three of five repetitions and generally requires more trials
before reaching a hit-free region. However, when BO succeeds, it can continue
improving the objective and finds the lowest observed 50-trial result.
LLM-based search reaches hit-free best candidates in four of five repetitions
and usually does so early, at trial 3, 3, 15, or 10. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}, this appears as an early drop in the best-so-far
objective followed by local refinement. In the remaining LLM-based repetition,
the agent failed to find a hit-free candidate early, treated the one-contact
region as the practical performance limit, and refined candidates within that
regime through the remaining budget.
Thus, in the five evaluated repetitions, BO was stronger at
discovering lower-objective candidates when exploration reached a useful
region, whereas LLM-based search exhibited earlier feasibility discovery.

\subsection{Best-Run Tracking Behavior}
\label{subsec:best_run_behavior}

Table~\ref{tab:best_trials} compares the best run obtained by each method.
For random search, the best candidate within the same budget still has one

\subsection{Hit-Free Weight-Space Patterns}
\label{subsec:weight_space_patterns}

The repeated runs reveal that the methods differ not only in final objective
value, but also in the type of hit-free weight settings they discover: multiple
weight combinations can complete the same slalom maneuver while lying in
different local regions of the objective landscape.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/fig4_hit_free_regions.pdf}
\caption{Representative projections of hit-free candidates in the log-scaled MPC
weight space. Markers R1--R6 denote representative solutions.}
\label{fig:local_regions}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:local_regions} projects all observed hit-free candidates from
LHC, BO, and LLM-based search onto four representative two-dimensional views of
the log-scaled weight space. The green shaded ellipse is an empirical summary of
the LLM-based hit-free candidates. It shows that the LLM repeatedly returns to a
high-heading region with moderate-to-high lateral and heading tracking weights
and moderate steering smoothing. This pattern is consistent with a physically
intuitive tuning strategy: emphasize tracking errors and avoid excessively
aggressive steering changes.

BO exhibits a different pattern. Its hit-free candidates are sparse, but they
appear in separated parts of the search space rather than only within the
LLM-based region. In particular, BO identifies a sharp high-heading candidate
with very weak steering penalties and several lower-heading alternatives with
different steering-effort and steering-rate trade-offs. LHC finds one hit-free
candidate in a similar low-heading portion of the space. These observations
indicate that the slalom objective contains several hit-free local regions, some
of which are less aligned with the intuitive high-heading strategy favored by
LLM-based search.

repeatedly exploits one plausible controller family, whereas BO exposes more
diverse and less intuitive hit-free candidates.

As a non-adaptive landscape analysis, Sobol sampling provides additional evidence
for the sparsity of hit-free weights. Among 4096 Sobol samples, only seven were
hit-free, corresponding to approximately 0.17\% of the sampled search space.
These hit-free Sobol samples belonged to low-heading or moderate-heading
families, consistent with the non-LLM candidates found by LHC and BO. Although
this analysis does not characterize the full feasible set, it indicates that
hit-free samples are rare under uniform sampling of the four-dimensional
log-scaled weight space.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studied simulator-in-the-loop MPC weight calibration for autonomous
vehicle path-following under a common 50-trial budget. The results show
complementary search behavior: LLM-based search reached hit-free
controller settings early in most repetitions, whereas BO discovered
lower-objective and less intuitive weight regions when its exploration
succeeded. The Sobol-based landscape analysis further shows a low hit-free
fraction under uniform sampling of the four-dimensional log-scaled search
space. The reported behavior is based on five repetitions of a single
CarMaker-Simulink slalom benchmark and should be read as an exploratory
comparison rather than general performance across driving conditions. Future
work will evaluate broader scenarios, friction conditions, larger repetition
counts, and exploration-preserving LLM--BO designs.
